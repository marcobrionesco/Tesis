III. SCIENTIFIC SUMMARY/ RESUMEN CIENTÍFICO (The maximum length for this section is 4 pages) 
 
(English Version) 
 
Fruit tree crops represent a significant capital for Chile. Particularly, over the last five years, there has been 
sustained growth in sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.) cultivation nationwide, with the Maule Region having the largest 
planted area. Increasing the planted area requires more water for irrigation. However, the alteration of the 
hydrological cycle, with a higher occurrence of extreme events such as droughts, is an effect of climate change. In 
Chile, drought events became common during the last decade, affecting surface and groundwater availability. 
Therefore, in this scenario of agricultural expansion, particularly of sweet cherries, and with reduced water 
availability, it is necessary to explore alternative water sources, with the reuse of treated wastewater being an 
underexplored option in the country. In this line, there has recently been a trend towards using less complex and 
more environmentally friendly technologies to improve wastewater quality in rural environments, where Nature-
based solutions (NbS) have been proposed for this purpose. Complementarily, international markets demand 
products from more sustainable agriculture, where the environmental impacts of their production process are 
known. 
 
Therefore, considering the above, this research proposal aims to evaluate the production of sweet cherry irrigated 
with wastewater treated by a type of NbS called treatment wetland (TW), focusing on crop yield, fruit quality, and 
environmental impacts, under climate change context. To this end, a three-year work plan is proposed, which will 
begin with a characterization of three alternative wastewater sources: a) domestic wastewater from homes and 
offices, b) wastewater generated from the warehouse, where agrochemical resources are washed in their 
application and, c) the water provided by the “Batuco” estuary, which emerge from the city of Linares, Maule 
region, Chile and where effluents from rural wastewater treatment plants and agricultural runoff are disposed. 
Subsequently, an experimental system will be implemented near the city of Linares (Maule Region) that combines 
the NbS technology of the TWs to treat wastewater and sweet cherry orchards, which will be irrigated with the 
treated water. During the operation of the TWs, water quality, plant development, and changes in the associated 
microbiology will be evaluated in relation to changes in operation. In parallel, four measurements are proposed to 
understand the development of sweet cherry plants: biometric measurements, plant tissue and soil nutrition, 
proximal and remote sensing from images, and the estimation of water consumption. Furthermore, to evaluate the 
quality of the fruit harvested, productivity analyses in weight/surface units, weight loss, hardness, color, total 
soluble solids, titratable acidity and caliber, will be carried out, along with food safety analyses. The operation of 
the TWs and the experimental cultivation orchards will be environmentally evaluated using life cycle assessment 
(LCA) methodology, initially estimating the impacts derived from the current cultivation management and then, 
evaluating the effects of the new water sources. Complementary to the above, regional characterization factors 
will be developed, using the AWARE method (water scarcity impact) to advance the precision of the water footprint 
assessment, to optimize water use. The socio-economic dimension of the implementation of the WT will also be 
analysed. 
 
With the development of this proposal, it is expected to obtain information on the feasibility of using new water 
sources for irrigation, considering NbS technologies as part of this process. Furthermore, it is expected to achieve 
a greater understanding of the environmental impacts derived from the implementation of the NbS to water reuse, 
and the potential benefits or drawbacks of this technology. Thus, the results may be useful in the development of 
public policies and serve as scientific evidence for future regulations, to have agriculture with sustainable growth 
within the new framework of reduced water availability imposed by climate change.

cinco años, ha habido un crecimiento sostenido en el cultivo de cerezo dulce (Prunus avium L.) a nivel nacional,

de eventos extremos como las sequías, es un efecto del cambio climático. En Chile, los eventos de sequía se han 
vuelto comunes durante la última década, afectando la disponibilidad de agua superficial y subterránea. Por lo 
tanto, en este escenario de expansión agrícola, particularmente del cerezo dulce, y con una menor disponibilidad 
hídrica, es necesario explorar fuentes alternativas de agua, siendo la reutilización de aguas residuales tratadas una 
opción poco explorada en el país. En esta línea, recientemente ha habido una tendencia hacia el uso de tecnologías 
menos complejas y más amigables con el medio ambiente para mejorar la calidad de las aguas residuales en 
entornos rurales, donde se han propuesto las Soluciones Basadas en la Naturaleza (SbN) con este propósito. 
Complementariamente, los mercados internacionales exigen productos provenientes de una agricultura más 
sostenible, donde se conozcan los impactos ambientales de su proceso productivo. 
 
Por lo tanto, considerando lo anterior, esta propuesta de investigación tiene como objetivo evaluar la producción 
de cerezo dulce irrigado con aguas residuales tratadas mediante un tipo de SbN denominado humedal de 
tratamiento (HT), enfocándose en el rendimiento del cultivo, la calidad del fruto y los impactos ambientales, en un 
contexto de cambio climático. Para ello, se propone un plan de trabajo de tres años, que comenzará con la 
caracterización de tres fuentes alternativas de aguas residuales: a) aguas residuales domésticas provenientes de 
viviendas y oficinas, b) aguas residuales generadas en la bodega donde se lavan los recursos agroquímicos tras su 
aplicación y, c) el agua provista por el estero “Batuco”, que emerge desde la ciudad de Linares, en la Región del 
Maule, Chile, y donde se vierten efluentes de plantas rurales de tratamiento de aguas residuales y escorrentías 
agrícolas. Posteriormente, se implementará un sistema experimental cerca de la ciudad de Linares (Región del 
Maule), que combinará la tecnología SbN de los HT para tratar las aguas residuales y huertos de cerezos dulces, los 
cuales serán irrigados con el agua tratada. Durante la operación de los HT, se evaluará la calidad del agua, el 
desarrollo de las plantas y los cambios en la microbiología asociada, en relación con los cambios operacionales. En 
paralelo, se proponen cuatro mediciones para comprender el desarrollo de las plantas de cerezo dulce: mediciones 
biométricas, nutrición del tejido vegetal y del suelo, teledetección proximal y remota mediante imágenes, y 
estimación del consumo hídrico. Además, para evaluar la calidad del fruto cosechado, se realizarán análisis de 
productividad en unidades de peso/superficie, pérdida de peso, dureza, color, sólidos solubles totales, acidez 
titulable y calibre, junto con análisis de inocuidad alimentaria. La operación de los HT y de los huertos 
experimentales será evaluada ambientalmente utilizando la metodología de Análisis de Ciclo de Vida (ACV), 
estimando inicialmente los impactos derivados de la gestión actual del cultivo y luego, evaluando los efectos del 
uso de nuevas fuentes de agua. De forma complementaria, se desarrollarán factores de caracterización regionales, 
utilizando el método AWARE (impacto por escasez hídrica), para avanzar en la precisión de la evaluación de la huella 
hídrica, con el fin de optimizar el uso del agua. También se analizará la dimensión socioeconómica de la 
implementación de los HT. 
 
Con el desarrollo de esta propuesta, se espera obtener información sobre la viabilidad del uso de nuevas fuentes 
de agua para riego, considerando las tecnologías SbN como parte de este proceso. Asimismo, se espera lograr una 
mayor comprensión de los impactos ambientales derivados de la implementación de las SbN para la reutilización 
de agua, y los beneficios o desventajas potenciales de esta tecnología. Así, los resultados podrían ser útiles para el 
desarrollo de políticas públicas y servir como evidencia científica para futuras regulaciones, con el fin de contar con 
una agricultura de crecimiento sostenible dentro del nuevo marco de disponibilidad hídrica reducida impuesto por 
el cambio climático.

2. JUSTIFICATION OF THEMES SELECTIONS (Maximum extension 1 page) 
This research proposal focuses on the thematic line B, "Agriculture in Contexts of Water Scarcity," specifically 
addressing the "Challenges to ensure Water Quantity and Quality." This theme was selected based on the following 
key considerations: 
 
Firstly, agriculture holds significant importance for Chile, particularly in the central regions (Maule, Ñuble, and 
Biobío), where institutions – principal and associated- are located. This proposal specifically examines sweet cherry 
cultivation, a sector that has experienced rapid growth in the past five years. Notably, exports of sweet cherries 
during the 2023/2024 season generated over US$ 2.5 billion in sales. The Maule Region, the proposed site for 
experimental work, boasts 32,801 hectares dedicated to this crop, representing 42% of the total cultivated area 
nationwide and making it the region with the most extensive sweet cherry cultivation. 
 
Secondly, Chile is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, being identified as one of the world's most 
affected countries. A significant consequence of climate change is the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme 
weather events, such as droughts, directly impacting water resource availability. The severe "Mega-drought" that 
affected the country between 2010 and 2020 demonstrated the vulnerability of water resources. This situation 
underscores the urgent need to explore alternative water sources to mitigate the future limitations on agricultural 
expansion due to water scarcity. Reusing treated wastewater presents a promising and reliable alternative water 
source, especially considering the anticipated increase in wastewater production from domestic sources, crop 
processing (industrial wastewater from packing facilities), and agricultural runoff, which can further pollute natural 
water bodies. However, research on the application and effects of alternative irrigation water sources on plants, 
crops, and overall production in Chile and the Maule Region remains limited. Therefore, acquiring robust data in 
this area is crucial. Implementing a suitable treatment technology is a prerequisite for wastewater reuse. Given the 
predominantly rural setting of agricultural activities, nature-based solutions (NbS) offer a convenient treatment 
alternative. This proposal will specifically evaluate the efficacy of NbS. Complementing the exploration of new water

V. PROJECT DESCRIPTION. Maximum extension 20 pages (do not include appendices). 
 
Research formulation:  
 
1. Relevance of discipline 
The world's population is expected to reach 10 billion by 2050 (United Nations, 2019), facing challenges such as 
securing food and water supplies, increasing energy demand, preserving natural ecosystems, and mitigating global 
environmental impacts, e.g., the events derived from climate change. Food systems have significantly increased 
production due to population growth based on intensive practices, which have led to negative environmental 
effects, including excessive use of agrochemicals and unsustainable use of water and energy, water and air 
pollution, loss of biodiversity, and risks to human health (Rasul et al., 2021). Food systems are responsible for one-
third of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and 30% of the world's total energy consumption 
(Crippa et al., 2021; Priyadarshini et al., 2021). By 2050, an estimated 80% increase in global GHG emissions from 
food production and arable land use, along with micronutrient deficiencies and increased incidence of non-
communicable diseases (Tilman and Clark, 2014). According to IPCC (2007), the most relevant agriculture-derived 
GHG emissions are carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O). The GHG emissions accounting 
begins with raw materials production, while in the cultivation stage, several studies establish that the main sources 
of GHGs correspond to the use of pesticides, fertilizers, and machines (Alaphilippe et al., 2013). Moreover, fertilizers 
contribute to groundwater contamination, eutrophication, and environmental acidification due to the volatilization 
of nitrogen in the form of ammonia (Quirós et al., 2014). In this context, fertigation—a technique that involves 
applying water-soluble fertilizers through irrigation—can exacerbate these impacts if not properly managed, by 
promoting nutrient overload in ecosystems (Muñoz et al., 2008). 
 
On the other hand, demanding about 85% of the global surface and groundwater sources, agriculture is the primary 
user of freshwater (Zulfiqar et al., 2021), which motivates - as a priority - the development of planning and 
management strategies of available water resources in the agriculture sector. According to the latest 
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) report, the global hydrological cycle is altered by the effects of 
climate change, so extreme events (e.g., droughts) are expected to occur more frequently. In Chile, drought events 
have been increasingly common during the past decade, affecting the availability of both surface and groundwater 
resources (Fernánez and Gironás et al., 2021). By 2021 in Chile, according to the technical criteria established by 
the General Water Directorate (DGA) of the Ministry of Public Works (MOP), 45% of the national population and 
49% of Chile's municipalities in the regions of Los Lagos, Maule, O'Higgins, Metropolitan, Valparaíso, Coquimbo, 
and Atacama were in a drought situation (DGA, 2021). In 2024, the DGA updated the cadaster, adding the Ñuble 
region and 58 localities in a situation of water scarcity (DGA, 2024). Moreover, temperatures in the Andes Mountain 
chain have risen by 0.2 to 0.3 ºC since 1976, and glaciers have retreated rapidly, making access to water difficult in 
rural areas (Naciones Unidas, 2021). The problem of water scarcity threatens the viability of irrigated agriculture, 
food processing industries, and the ecological status of wetlands, motivating decision-makers and communities to 
address the increasingly necessary adaptation (Lesslie et al., 2023). Furthermore, water scarcity also represents a 
commercial barrier for exporting countries (like Chile) when the product is dependent on water supply (e.g., fruit 
market) due to the growing consumer demand for environmental product certification (Pereira et al., 2020). 
 
The barrier represented by the reduction in water availability for agricultural production will affect Chile, especially 
in locations where agriculture plays a relevant role in the economy (Leiva et al. 2025). The Maule Region, located 
in the central part of the country, is one of these regions (ODEPA 2019). One of the important agricultural activities 
is fruit cultivation. According to the ODEPA-CIREN Fruit Cadaster 2024, the national area under fruit trees is 
estimated at 372,576 ha. At a regional level, the O'Higgins Region has the highest proportion in the country, with 
26.8% of the total, followed by the Maule Region with 24.4%. Among the fruit species cultivated, sweet cherry trees 
ranked first in surface area, representing 19% of the national total, followed by walnut (11.6%), European hazelnut 
(9.8%) and table grapevine (8.9%).

In this context, the estimated national area of sweet cherry trees amounts to 76,447.40 ha, consolidating its position 
as the species with the most significant expansion in the last five years. Thus, sweet cherry production in Chile has 
positioned itself as one of the country's main fruit species in terms of cultivated surface area and export volume. 
The 2023/24 fruit season set a record for Chile, with exports reaching 9.3 billion dollars FOB (Free on Board), the 
highest value in the last 10 years and a 13.1% increase over the previous season. Fresh fruit accounted for 74.9% 
of total exports, with sweet cherries as the main species by value, with shipments of 3.1 billion dollars FOB, 19.9% 
more than the previous season. This scenario reaffirms the economic relevance of this crop in national cultivation 
and its strategic role in the context of Chilean fruit and vegetable exports. Sweet Cherry production is mainly 
concentrated in the Maule and O'Higgins regions, which account for 42.91% and 42.30% of the national surface 
area, respectively. In terms of planted areas, Maule leads with 32,801.42 ha, an average yield of 9.6 tons per ha 
and the highest density in the country, with 1,164 plants per ha. Therefore, in the context of the expansion of sweet 
cherry cultivation in the country and Maule Region, with its associated water necessities, it is necessary to explore 
alternative water sources to address future irrigation water scarcity problems. Among the different alternative 
water resources, treated wastewater reuse must be considered. However, treated wastewater reuse in Chile is still 
an emerging topic (Vera-Puerto et al., 2022). This situation differs from that of southern Europe and Israel, places 
with vast experience on this topic and a climate similar to the Maule Region (Lavrnic et al., 2017; Oron et al., 2014). 
Additionally, it has been concluded that wastewater reuse represents a cost-competitive alternative to seawater 
desalination as an alternative water resource (Crutchik et al., 2021). Therefore, advancing new approaches that 
combine irrigation with treated wastewater (intrinsic fertigation) is crucial for the field. These new approaches 
should move beyond traditional wastewater treatments, such as activated sludge, and explore more 
environmentally friendly technologies such as Nature-based Solutions (NbS). 
 
Another important strategy to address water scarcity is optimising water use for agricultural production. To advance 
in this topic, in 2014, the water footprint norm was published, referred to as ISO 14046:2014 (ISO, 2014), which 
establishes a life cycle perspective when the water footprint of a product is measured. This norm defines water 
scarcity as the consumption (by humans and ecosystems) that approaches or exceeds a water body’s natural 
regeneration capacity (i.e., availability in a region). The Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a standardized methodology 
(ISO, 2006) that estimates multiple environmental impact indicators, such as carbon footprint or water footprint, 
by using characterization factors (CF), linking emissions or resource use to a certain environmental impact category 
(ISO, 2006). From the impact methods recommended by UNEP/SETAC, the Available WAter REmaining (AWARE) 
method estimates the water scarcity category, representing the remaining water available once human and 
ecosystem demands have been met. However, previous studies on the regionalization of the AWARE method in 
Brazil (Pereira et al., 2020), Australia (Bontinck et al., 2021), and Peru (Sanchez-Matos et al., 2023) highlight the 
need to develop regionalized CFs due to significant differences from the original (i.e., global) CFs of the method. 
Therefore, developing characterization factors in the Chilean context for calculating water scarcity impacts is 
essential to improve water management in agricultural systems. In this regard, it is relevant to support farmers, 
through scientific evidence, to develop sustainable strategies in the agricultural sector, since environmental studies 
on fruits are still in demand (Djekic et al., 2018). 
 
Therefore, considering the above, this project proposes the application of Nature-based Solutions (NbS) as 
treatment systems to generate an alternative water resource – treated wastewater – for sweet cherry tree 
irrigation. Complementary, this research will evaluate the impact of this approach on crop yield and fruit quality, as 
well as its environmental impacts through a LCA perspective. 
 
2. State of the art of the research subject 
 
2.1. Nature-based solutions (NbS) for wastewater treatment and their effluent reuse in irrigation 
NbS can be defined as technical solutions that mimic and are supported by natural processes, which are cost-
effective, provide environmental, social, and economic benefits, as well as helping to increase resilience (Vera- 


=== PÁG 10 ===
 
 
 
Puerto et al., 2020). Accordingly, NbS has several technologies that can effectively treat wastewater. NbS uses 
plants, soil, bacteria, and other natural elements and processes to remove pollutants from wastewater, thus 
reducing chemical and energy inputs. These approaches can treat wastewater in a sustainable, low-cost, low-impact 
manner and can be designed to have a long performance lifespan (Cross et al., 2021). Technologies such as 
treatment wetlands (TWs) have been representative of NbS technologies for wastewater treatment, and they have 
been used successfully since the 80s and 90s in different countries (Brix and Arias, 2005; Molle et al., 2005; Nivala 
et al., 2018). TWs are engineered wetlands that are designed and constructed to mimic natural wetland systems 
for treating wastewater. These systems, mainly comprised of vegetation, substrates, microorganisms and water, 
use complex processes involving physical, chemical, and biological mechanisms to remove various contaminants 
from wastewater (Wu et al., 2015). Depending on flow direction and water level, TWs are classified into three types: 
a) free water surface (FWS), b) horizontal subsurface flow (HF), and c) vertical subsurface flow (VF) (Fonder and 
Headly, 2013). TWs with subsurface flow (HF and VF) have been employed successfully for secondary and tertiary 
treatment, reducing the risk of exposure of contaminants during treatment compared to FWS (Dotro et al., 2017). 
Currently, VF is becoming an alternative to traditional HF, which is the most used TW type worldwide (Moreira and 
Dias, 2020; Rodriguez-Dominguez et al., 2020). VF TWs are becoming more common than HF TWs because of their 
potential to cope with higher organic loadings (>6 gBOD5/[m2-d]), Dotro et al., (2017)), capacity to nitrify 
(ammonium removal above 80% (Brix and Arias, 2005), and smaller footprint demand (VF TWs, 2–4 m2/Inhab [Dotro 
et al., 2017]; HF TWs, 5 m2/Inhab, [Vymazal, 2011]). Additionally, VF TWs have more design and operational 
parameters (Bohórquez et al., 2017) compared with HF TWs; therefore, the adaptability of the VF TWs to 
operational conditions makes it more suitable as a system to be implemented for wastewater treatment and 
adapted to socioeconomic conditions of Latin-America (Vera-Puerto et al., 2021b).  
 
VF TWs with intermittent application have been proven effective for solids removal (measured as Total Suspended 
Solids [TSS]) and organic matter removal (measured as 5-day biological oxygen demand [BOD5] or chemical oxygen 
demand [COD]) exhibiting efficiencies of 50-95 % when applied in domestic wastewater (Brix and Arias, 2005; Vera-
Puerto et al., 2021b; Tsihrintzis, 2017). However, removal efficiency is lower (usually below 60%) in the case of total 
nitrogen (TN) and specially for total phosphorus (TP), since most processes in these systems do not eliminate 
nitrogen entirely but rather transform it into different forms (Kadlec et al., 2005). Nitrification has been identified 
as the predominant process in these systems due to their high oxygen transfer rates (20–90 g O₂/m²·d), which 
support aerobic redox potentials (>+100 mV) and favorable conditions for nitrifying bacteria (Soti et al., 2022). 
Reported ammonium-to-nitrate conversion efficiencies range from 63% to 73%, with effluent ammonium 
concentrations below 5 mg/L (Pelissari et al., 2014; Leiva et al., 2017). The conversion of NH₄⁺ to NO₃⁻ offers a 
significant advantage for agricultural reuse, as nitrate is a plant-available form of nitrogen that enhances the 
agronomic value of treated water for irrigation applications. For pathogens, VF TWs are reported to remove only 1-
3 Log-Units of pathogens (fecal coliforms or Escherichia Coli [E. Coli]) (Zurita et al., 2015).  
 
The feasibility of reusing treated water through the different types of TWs has been widely evaluated, highlighting 
positive effects on plant growth, nutrient supply, and soil quality, but specific information regarding its application 
in fruit production remains limited (Almuktar et al., 2018). Nan et al. (2020) established that single-stage TWs (HF 
or VF) combined with additional steps (for example, disinfection) could reach the European Union standards for 
agricultural wastewater reuse. Recently, Vera-Puerto et al. (2024) also suggest that VF TWs can fulfill the 
requirements for agricultural reuse, considering several international regulations, but they mentioned that VF TWs’ 
size is important for this achievement. Therefore, the foregoing demonstrates the feasibility of generating irrigation 
water for agricultural products from wastewater treated by TWs, and for the advantages discussed, in this project 
VF TWs will be the NbS technology selected to be applied.

2.2. Environmental life-cycle assessment of irrigated water systems and constructed wetlands  
Various studies have addressed the environmental assessment of irrigated water systems in the agriculture sector, 
mainly focusing on comparing irrigation practices and the use of reclaimed water. The water-energy‑carbon nexus 
framework was evaluated in a wheat production system under conventional irrigation (CI), sprinkler irrigation (SI), 
and drip irrigation (DI) technology, identifying that SI and CI effectively mitigated water degradation and reduced 
water consumption, but at the cost of increased energy consumption and carbon emissions (Liu et al., 2024). In 
maize cultivation, flood- and drip-irrigated systems were analyzed to evaluate nitrogen and carbon footprints, 
where grain yields with flood irrigation and optimized nitrogen management, incorporating urease inhibitor, 
obtained a higher yield than without using this component (Di et al., 2024). Additionally, desalinated seawater 
demonstrated environmental benefits in the organic grapefruit system, combined using of ion exchange resins for 
deboronation, compared to reverse osmosis (Abdallah et al., 2023). The comparison of irrigation systems, such as 
center pivot and drip, reveals that pipes and filters had the most significant impact on the water and carbon 
footprints, with galvanized steel pipes and sand filters representing the hotspots (Angnes et al., 2023). Similarly, the 
environmental impacts of future irrigation requirements for potato cultivation in Cyprus are estimated to have a 
potential carbon emission of about 1369.4 tons CO2eq (Litskas et al., 2023). Finally, the comparison of rainfed 
(baseline), Decision Support System (DSS)-based irrigation practices, and farmer experience-based (conventional) 
irrigation practices in the olive orchard, demonstrated that the DSS-based irrigation management could reduce 
water and energy use by 42.1% compared to conventional practices (Fotia et al., 2021). 
 
Regarding reusing water in agricultural systems, wastewater reuse from NbS such as lagoons reduces carbon 
emissions, primarily through excavation reductions, reduces fertilizer use, and increases crop yields at previously 
rainfed sites (Thompson and Dvorak, 2024). In addition, the adoption of reclaimed water (from the farmhouse) 
through a TW system for irrigation, together with the application of deficit irrigation strategies in an olive grove, 
demonstrated that water saving of up to 20-50% can be obtained (Vanella et al., 2023). Furthermore, the 
combination of life cycle costing with a monetized LCA identified internal and external environmental costs of 
tomato, table grape, and artichoke cultivation with reclaimed water, where crop cultivation with reclaimed water 
could result in environmental benefits of higher value than cost (Canaj and Mehmeti, 2022). While less prevalent 
than in wastewater treatment or waste management strategies, LCA methodology has been applied in the literature 
to evaluate TWs, urban and industrial wastewater treatment, and sludge TWs. In the case of TWs for urban 
wastewater, the aim of these studies has been primarily to compare two or more wastewater treatment systems 
(i.e., TW coupled with other types of technology), and compare the performance of different configurations of TWs 
(i.e., HF-TW vs VF-TW), or analyze the performance of the TWs (Ziegler-Rodriguez & Garfí, 2025). In the Chilean 
context, a study performed by Carrillo et al. (2023) analyzed the effect that different plant configurations 
(monoculture and polyculture) and seasonal conditions (cold and warm season) have over the environmental 
performance of pilot-scale HF-TW, observing lower impacts during the cold seasons and an environmental trade-
off between climate change and eutrophication depending on culture conditions. A previous study by Casas-Ledón 
et al. (2017) showed that seasonality can also significantly affect the environmental remediation costs of HF-TW, 
measured in terms of the exergy needed by the system.  
 
Regarding the use of TWs for sludge treatment, these systems dewater the sludge and stabilize it through natural 
processes, producing a final product, which can be used as fertilizer for agricultural purposes (Brix, 2017; Nielsen, 
2003), where the application of LCA to this type of wetland in literature is limited with only three studies in Europe 
addressing this environmental assessment on different sludge treatment scenarios (Larsen et al., 2018; Uggetti et 
al., 2011) and the carbon emissions of two TWs in alternate locations (Uggetti et al., 2012). Five studies have 
addressed the use of TWs for industrial wastewater treatment. Two studies have been aimed at analyzing a specific 
technology for the treatment of industrial effluents (DiMuro et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2018), while others at 
comparing different TWs approaches (Alam et al., 2023) or other technologies (Flores et al., 2019, 2020). Alam et 
al. (2023), who considered hybrid VF and HF, identified energy-intensive materials like concrete as major impact 
drivers. Alam et al. (2023) also found that VF-TWs were more environmentally efficient for treating Industrial

5.2. Research line 1: Nature-based solutions applications (SO1) 
The NbS technology to be employed for testing will be TWs, specifically VF-TWs. Prior to the final design, a three-
month monitoring period will be conducted for the three water sources to be tested (Figure 1). Grab samples will 
be collected every two weeks for water quality analysis, totaling six monitoring campaigns. Sampling points will be: 
a) domestic wastewater effluent from housing and administrative offices, b) wastewater coming from the 
agrochemical warehouse, at the discharge point before the storage tank, and c) the Batuco estuary, where a 
composite sample will be taken from three points located 1 meter from the riverbank, near the designated 
extraction site. Samples will be analyzed for pH, temperature (T), electrical conductivity (EC), oxidation-reduction 
potential (ORP), turbidity (TU), 5-day biochemical oxygen demand (BOD₅), chemical oxygen demand (COD), total 
suspended solids (TSS), ammonium (NH₄⁺), nitrate (NO₃⁻), nitrite (NO2⁻), total nitrogen (TN), phosphate (PO₄³⁻), 
total phosphorus (TP), and Fecal Coliforms as a pathogen indicator. The water quality analyses will be performed at 
the "Laboratory of Water Quality" at UCM in Talca and the Laboratories of UST in Concepción. Concurrently, water 
consumption in the housing, administrative office, and agrochemical warehouse will be monitored over the same 
three-month period to establish the expected wastewater production for treatment system sizing. A survey 
methodology similar to the work of Yevenes (2019) will be applied to determine water quantities. 
 
After the three-month monitoring period, the required surface area for the VF-TWs will be determined. Initially, 
the size of the VF-TW units will be established for domestic wastewater. This design will be based on design models 
and loadings from Dotro et al. (2017), incorporating construction modifications proposed by Vera-Puerto et al. 
(2021a) to adapt international VF-TW models for Chilean conditions. Subsequently, the same VF-TW size will be 
utilized to treat water from the Batuco estuary and agrochemical warehouse. For these two cases, the applied water 
quantity will be determined to work with the same loading characteristics as those used for domestic wastewater. 
The operation will be divided into two phases based on the applied loading rates to the VF-TWs: a) Phase 1: a COD 
loading rate of 30 gCOD/(m²·d) and an associated ammonium loading rate of approximately 3 gNH4+/(m2-d); b) 
Phase2: a COD loading rate of 60gCOD/(m2-d) and its associated ammonium rate of approximately 6 gNH4+/(m2-d). 
These loading rates were selected based on values reported in Dotro et al. (2017) and Vera-Puerto et al. (2023),

considering that COD removal efficiency might drop 70% below at loading rates above 40 gCOD/(m²·d). However, 
the increased loading in Phase 2 aims to evaluate whether the applied water quantity can be increased without 
significantly compromising water quality. Phase 1 will be implemented for year 1 and 2. Phase 2 will be implemented 
for year 3. This duration for each phase is representative of the annual cycle of the plants and will allow for the 
assessment of the VF-TWs' performance across all four seasons and its influence on three sweet cherry cultivation

procedures outlined in Metcalf and Eddy (2004). The treatment objective will be to meet the quality standards 
specified in NCh3456 (INN, 2022) related to wastewater reuse for irrigation. For the other two water sources, the 
Batuco estuary and the agrochemical warehouse effluent, the necessity of a disinfection system will be evaluated 
based on the findings of the initial three-month monitoring period.

to December, corresponding to months 9 to 12 in the Gantt chart) and the subsequent period (January to April, 
corresponding to months 1 to 4 in the Gantt chart), water samples will be collected every three weeks; b) during 
non-irrigation periods (May to August, corresponding to months 5 to 8 in the Gantt chart), water samples will be 
collected every four weeks. Samples will be taken at both the inlet and outlet of the VF TWs, besides after 
disinfection system (in case of employment). The water quality parameters analyzed will be consistent with those 
proposed during the initial three-month monitoring period. Complementary, water quantity will be evaluated. 
 
For the VF-TWs, Typha spp. will be implemented as the plant species. This species is traditionally employed in TWs 
(Kumar et al., 2024). Several biometric characteristics will be measured every three weeks in six individual plants 
within each VF-TW across experimentation time to assess plant adaptation. These measurements will include shoot 
production, the number of leaves produced, the number of shoots, stem height, and stem thickness. Additionally, 
leaf chlorophyll status will be assessed every three weeks. Once flowering begins, the number of flowers will be 
recorded weekly. After Phases 1 and 2, aboveground plant biomass samples will be collected from the six individuals 
of each VF-TWs for tissue content analysis (N, P, K). Biomass determination will be conducted in accordance with 
plant development stages (Vera et al., 2014). Furthermore, plant physiological status will be evaluated using 
proximal sensing tools, specifically the Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI) (Jackson et al., 1981) and the Normalized 
Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) (Rouse et al., 1974). The CWSI will be calculated from thermal infrared (TIR) 
images of the plant canopies. These images, comprising numerous surface temperature (Ts) pixels, will be exported 
to comma-separated values (CSV) files and subsequently processed using a dedicated algorithm (Carrasco-
Benavides et al., 2020). The NDVI will be derived from canopy reflectance data captured by a multi-spectral camera 
operating in the visible and near-infrared wavelengths. Multi-spectral images will be acquired twice per growing 
season (mid-season and end-of-season). 
 
Complementary to the previous analysis, a microbiological study focusing on the metabolic profiling of microbial 
communities within the three VF-TWs will be conducted using two complementary methods. First, direct 
inoculation of samples will be performed utilizing a BIOLOG system (detailed in the equipment section). Second, 
the metabolic potential of these microbial communities will be analyzed through bioinformatics profiling. This 
bacteriological analysis will be instrumental in identifying differences in bacterial development among the VF-TWs 
resulting from varying types of wastewater and applications. Samples will be collected from each VF-TW system via 
designated sampling ports. For analysis, 25 g samples of filter media will be aseptically collected from three 
randomly selected points at two distinct depths within each VF-TW: 0.3 meters from the top and 0.3 meters from 
the bottom. These samples will be obtained using sterile tweezers and immediately transferred to sterile Falcon 
tubes. Two sampling events will occur per operational phase (1 and 2): the first at six months and the second at 
twelve months following the start of the Phase. The extraction procedure for microbiological sampling from the 
filter media will adhere to the recommendations outlined in Ramírez-Vargas et al. (2020).

5.3. Research line 2: Agronomic and fruit analysis (SO2) 
The research will be conducted in a commercial sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.) orchard located in the commune of 
Linares, Maule Region, Chile (Palmilla, WGS 84 datum, 35°45'34.60"S, 71°45'24.34"W, 165 m.a.s.l.) (Figure 2). The 
area to be evaluated is approximately 1500-2,000 m2, with a planting density of 1250 plants per hectare (planting 
distance 4 x 2m between rows and plants, respectively) of the Lapins variety on sour cherry rootstock, with a central 
axis conduction system. A drip irrigation system will be used, with drippers placed 50 cm from each other, 
presenting a flow rate of 2 litres/hour. The final definition of plant quantity will be developed after knowing the 
capacity (in terms of water quantity) of VF TWs. The sweet cherry trees will be irrigated with the three new water 
sources proposed in this work, and for control, a set of plants will be irrigated with water from natural sources 
employed now for irrigation. 
 
Several measurement strategies will be developed to evaluate sweet cherry plant growth and evolution, and its 
influence on its production yield. The first will be biometric measurements, such as the cross-sectional area of the 
trunk (CSA) and number of branches and buds, to monitor plant growth. In parallel, chlorophyll measurements will 
be carried out in the field using the Konica Minolta SPAD 502 Plus portable meter during the trial period (September 
to April). Four individuals for each new water proposed will be tagged, and five leaves will be monitored. These 
measurements will be taken. The second strategy involves the evaluation of plant tissues and soil nutrition. The 
parameters C, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, Na, Cu, Fe, Mn, Zn, B, and S will be monitored at two points for each year in the 
experiment: a) at the middle of the year for soil, and b) at the end of the year for foliar analysis. Tissue analyses will 
follow the protocols described in Sadzawa et al. (2007). The third measurement will be the determination of 
parameters using proximal and remote sensing: a) leaf area index (LAI) (Bréda, 2003), b) infrared thermography and 
c) Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) (Rouse et al., 1974). The LAI is an indicator that accounts for the 
relationship between the surface area of plant cover (leaf area) and its relation to the surface area designated for 
plant growth (Bréda, 2003). This indicator describes the plant's vigour and can be monitored over time using 
allometric relationships. In this trial, the LAI will be obtained from the extraction of random samples of plants, with 
a minimum of 5 leaves extracted from three plants for each residual water source treatment. Subsequently, they 
will be photographed in the laboratory with a digital camera (generic) on a surface of a known area at a constant 
height and under controlled lighting conditions, as Carrasco-Benavides et al. (2016) indicated. The digital images 
obtained and after their conversion to B/W images and subsequent export to comma-separated text files (CSV) can 
be processed using data management software, obtaining the average LAI values. This data will allow allometric 
relationships to be established with the height and estimation data for determining the leaf area (LA) of all plants, 
in addition to the construction of evolution curves of this parameter throughout the study period. Regarding NDVI, 
this index has been widely used to quantify biomass or plant vigour (Huang et al., 2021). NDVI is calculated from 
the following relationship, considering the surface reflectance of the vegetation cover obtained with a radiometric 
sensor: 
 
NDVI = (ρNIR-ρR)/(ρNIR+ρR)     (Eq. 1) 
 
Where ρNIR and ρR are the corresponding surface reflectance values for the near-infrared (0.78-0.90 μm) and red

high NDVI value (≈1) indicates a healthy plant; conversely, NDVI values close to -1 indicate dead or nonexistent 
plants. Values greater than zero describe intermediate cases. This study will obtain vegetation cover reflectance 
values from the experimental plots using images captured by a multispectral camera (Micasense RedEdge, Seattle, 
USA) mounted on an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV). Flights will be conducted at least once a month, at solar noon

5.4. Research line 3: Sustainability assessment of SbN strategies in irrigated fruit farming (SO3) 
 
5.4.1. Environmental life cycle assessment of SbN strategies in irrigated fruit farms. The LCA is a standardized 
methodology, which consists of four steps: i) aim and scope, ii) inventory analysis, iii) impact assessment, and iv) 
interpretation. In the first step, the objective and system boundaries (e.g., cradle-to-gate), as well as the functional 
unit that represents the system’s function under study, are defined. For VF-TWs, the functional unit is defined as a 
quantity of input/output flow (i.e., wastewater or treated wastewater), and as the amount of to-treat/treated water 
equivalent for a determined population (i.e., person equivalents) (Ziegler-Rodriguez & Garfio, 2025). Then, the 
inventory analysis is carried out, corresponding to the data collection step, identifying different flows of the system 
(e.g., emissions, energy, and material consumptions, etc.), which will be built based on the experimental results of 
the pilot systems. Here, the GHG emissions will be measured using a Gasmet DX4015 portable gas analyzer for 
ambient air analysis, which uses Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) to determine GHG concentrations 
and fluxes. In the third step (i.e., impact assessment), the inventory collected is transformed into impact category 
indicators, following a midpoint (e.g., global warming, eutrophication, etc.) and endpoint (quantifying impacts on 
ecosystems, human health, and resources) approaches. The final step consists of identifying the hotspots of the 
analyzed system, and evaluation of results through completeness, sensitivity, consistency, and uncertainty analysis. 
The LCA will be carried out before the implementation of the NbS, to obtain the baseline scenario in terms of various 
impact indicators. Then, the LCA will be applied again after the implementation of the NbS to verify the 
environmental benefits or drawbacks of the technology in the cultivation of sweet cherries. Special attention will 
be focus on water-related impact categories such as freshwater eutrophication, ecotoxicity, and scarcity.

5.4.3. Socio-economic analysis of SbN strategy in irrigated fruit farms. The economic analysis of the NbS

viability of installing the technology, in a context of, for example, scaling up the operation. The aim is to determine 
and identify the potential economic barriers to adopting the NbS. This step will consider information such as 
equipment requirements (e.g., size, capacity, quantity required), economic indicators such as total capital 
investment (fixed and working capital investment), and related costs such as construction, operation, labour, 
maintenance, and utilities (i.e., energy). To estimate the economic feasibility, a discounted cash flow analysis will 
be carried out. In addition, the potential savings from avoiding or reducing the use of synthetic fertilisers, as well as 
the potential scalability of the system, will also be taken into account in the analysis. Regarding the socio-economic 
dimension, the operation and maintenance of TW do not demand specialized labour (Flores et al. 2023). The NbS

local economic development (Vymazal, 2014). However, no study has yet thoroughly assessed social issues

Research line 1 aims to achieve two primary objectives through the aforementioned cooperation: a) with national

partners, to extend the knowledge gained from this proposal beyond national borders (especially in years 2 and 3) 
and to secure support from two specialists for the validation of the experimental unit (year 1) and collaboration on 
scientific outputs. Research line 2, related to national and international cooperation, aims to achieve two main 
objectives regarding the effect of applying different water sources on the general health of sweet cherry trees: 1) 
to characterize physiology, nutritional status, and water balance, and 2) to evaluate fruit conditions concerning 
quality and yield parameters. Therefore, the contribution of the University of Melbourne, University of Santiago de 
Compostela and the University of Stellenbosch, internationally recognized in digital agriculture, will be essential for 
properly developing this research. Research line 3 aims to reach three main results based on both national and 
international cooperation: i) the environmental assessment of the implementation of the NbS in the sweet cherry 
cultivation to identify potential advantages and drawbacks through a sustainable perspective; ii) to develop regional 
characterization factors for the environmental analysis of other crop products elaborated in Chile, since currently 
the application of global factors in Chilean LCA studies are not well representative to the national context; iii) 
economic evaluation of the NbS implementation and the socio-economic analysis of the farmers' willingness to 
adopt this strategy. 
 
Outreach, dissemination of results and knowledge transfer to non-academic environments: The dissemination of 
research findings will be focused on scientific community and the broader community. For broader community will 
employ a dual-pronged strategy encompassing both online and in-person activities. Next, the dissemination 
strategy is explained: 
- Scientific dissemination: The scientific results will be disseminated in articles submitted to peer-reviewed 
international journals (preferent Q1 or Q2) indexed to WoS and/or Scopus, and in relevant associated national and 
international conferences. This project expects to publish at least six (6) articles, two associated with each research 
line. The beginning of the publishing plan is scheduled for the second year and is estimated to continue until the 
end of the project.  
- Online Activities: A dedicated project website will be designed and actively promoted as the central hub for online 
communication. This website will feature comprehensive project information, researcher profiles, baseline data, 
and regular updates on project activities. The primary language of the website will be Spanish, with a complete 
translation available in English. To further enhance outreach, the website will be linked to a newly established 
Instagram profile specifically for this project. This Instagram account will be updated at least weekly with engaging 
content. In addition to the website and social media presence, the project will produce a series of accessible fact 
sheets. During the first year, at least three fact sheets will be developed, employing clear and concise language to 
explain key concepts related to each research line. Building upon the project's outcomes, an additional three fact 
sheets will be created in the third year to disseminate the results. These fact sheets will be made available online 
as downloadable PDF documents and will also be produced as printed materials for distribution during in-person 
events. Furthermore, the project will involve the creation of professional-quality videos to document its progress 
and impact. At least three videos, one for each year of the project, will be produced. Complementing these, at least 
three short videos (under one minute in length) will be developed each semester, highlighting researchers, 
students, activities, and general project-related information. 
- In-Person Activities: To engage directly with the community, three workshops will be conducted, one in each year 
of the project. The first workshop, serving as a project launch event, will be held during the first semester. The 
second workshop is planned for the first semester of the second year and will focus on presenting the key findings

Application and/or Transfer of Generated Knowledge and Impact in Other (Non-Academic) Sectors:  The private 
sector partners involved in this proposal, Frutícola SABA SpA and Abud & Cia, have established relationships with a 
network of small producers of sweet cherry, who represent a prime target group for the transfer of knowledge and 
technology generated by the NbS-Waterry project upon its completion. During the project's execution, particularly 
in years 2 and 3, these small producers will be invited to visit the experimental site. These visits will be crucial, 
allowing them to witness firsthand the benefits of NbS implementation in a practical setting. To further support this 
knowledge transfer, producers will receive materials derived from LCA and water footprint analyses. The research 
staff will emphasize the significance of understanding these parameters for optimizing production and meeting 
future international environmental standards related to water use. Given that the NbS technology to be tested 
includes TWs, Humedal Chile, a company specializing in the design and construction of TWs for domestic 
wastewater, will be a key partner in facilitating the adoption of this technology among interested producers. 
Humedal Chile has expressed strong interest in this proposal as a strategic opportunity to broaden its product 
portfolio beyond domestic wastewater treatment. Their prior experience implementing TWs will be invaluable for 
the effective transfer of this technology. 
 
The academic institutions participating in this proposal—UCM, UBB, and UST—along with the research centers 
CEHUM and CEAZA, which have an interest in the project's outcomes, will be vital partners in disseminating the 
acquired knowledge and technology to the public sector. Like the engagement with private institutions, staff from 
CEHUM and CEAZA will be invited to visit the experimental site during year 2. Furthermore, during years 2 and 3, 
representatives from key public sector entities, specifically the Ministry of Agriculture, Ministry of Environment, 
Ministry of Health, CORFO, and INDAP, will also be invited to the experimental site. These public institutions will be 
informed about the advantages of employing NbS for producing treated wastewater as a source for irrigation. 
Additionally, during these visits, the project team will highlight the importance of the project's findings in informing 
potential regulations concerning the use of treated wastewater in agriculture. Currently, Chile has guidelines 
related to treated wastewater reuse but lacks specific regulations. Therefore, the results of this project will be 
significant in the discussion and formulation of new regulatory frameworks on reuse.
