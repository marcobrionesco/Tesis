Abstract
Efficient nitrogen (N) management in perennial fruit orchards is constrained by species-
specific differences in N demand and fruit N removal, which can result in distinct soil
N uptake patterns even under similar fertilization regimes. This study assessed whether
apple (Malus domestica Borkh.) and sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.) orchards differ in soil
mineral nitrogen (Nmin) composition by analyzing nitrate (NO3−) and ammonium (NH4+)
stocks in topsoil (0–20 cm). Soil samples were collected in spring from a long-term orchard
experiment over two consecutive years and from a commercial orchard on two sampling
dates in Germany. In apple orchards, Nmin was dominated by NO3−(83%), whereas
cherry orchards showed a more balanced composition (42% NO3−and 58% NH4+). These
patterns were consistent across years, sites, fertilization types (mineral or organic), and key
soil properties, including total organic carbon and total nitrogen, but were not explained by
these factors. The elevated NH4+ proportion in cherry soils suggests a species-associated
pattern in soil N dynamics. Overall, the results highlight species-associated differences
in soil Nmin composition between apple and sweet cherry orchards. Accounting for tree
species differences may therefore improve N management and enhance N use efficiency in
apple and sweet cherry production.
Keywords: plant–soil interactions; soil nitrogen dynamics; species-specific effects; perennial
fruit orchards; apple; sweet cherry; nitrogen use efficiency; nitrate; ammonium
1. Introduction
There is an increasing demand for high-quality fruit rich in health-promoting com-
pounds, produced under sustainable production conditions [1–3]. Nitrogen (N) is a key
nutrient in plant nutrition, playing a central role in amino acid and chlorophyll synthesis
and thus in photosynthetic performance [4,5]. Nitrogen deficiency in orchards leads to
reduced vegetative growth, leaf chlorosis, and poor fruit set, whereas excessive N supply
can delay fruit production, reduce fruit quality, and increase susceptibility to frost damage,
diseases, and pests [6,7]. In addition, under humid climatic conditions, excess nitrate is
prone to leaching into groundwater, posing significant environmental risks [8].
Although N deficiency is relatively rare in commercial fruit orchards [5,6], recom-
mendations for N fertilizer application rates vary widely between countries. For example,
annual application rates for the same apple cultivar range from approximately 80 kg N ha−1

pean Commission’s Green Deal and Farm-to-Fork strategies aim to reduce mineral fertilizer
use by 20% by 2030 [10]. Achieving this goal requires improved knowledge of soil N dynam-
ics under species-specific orchard conditions to support sustainable fertilization practices.
Perennial fruit crops such as apple and sweet cherry differ fundamentally from annual
cropping systems. In tree crops, up to 80% of total N demand can be supplied from internal
nutrient reserves accumulated in previous years, whereas current-season root uptake
typically accounts for the remaining 20% [11]. Consequently, successful N fertilization
must balance vegetative growth, fruit production, and the replenishment of perennial N
reserves while minimizing environmental losses [12]. Fertilizer strategies therefore need to
account for tree developmental stage and long-term nutrient cycling processes [13,14].
Some authors point to knowledge gaps regarding N fertilization in perennial orchards,
which are due to leaching, adsorption, and release of plant-available N during the biodegra-
dation of organic matter [15,16]. Further research also appears to be needed on nutrient
uptake by roots and nutrient storage in wood tissue [7,17]. Nitrogen uptake by orchard trees
is species-specific [18], yet the precise mechanisms linking N form and quantity to growth
are still not fully understood [19]. Consequently, species-specific differences in N uptake
between apple and cherry may also be reflected indirectly in soil N dynamics [20,21].
Sweet cherry trees appear to require more N per unit of fruit harvested than apple
trees, with reported values ranging from 0.14–0.44 kg N per 100 kg of cherry fruit com-
pared with 0.05–0.16 kg N per 100 kg of apple fruit [22]. Although average apple yields
(40–60 t ha−1) [23,24] typically exceed those of sweet cherry (12–20 t ha−1) [25], the higher
N concentration in cherry fruit suggests species-specific physiological differences in relation
to N uptake. It is therefore plausible that such differences are reflected in contrasting soil
N dynamics, particularly in the temporal patterns and composition of mineral nitrogen
(Nmin) in the root zone.
Given the limited understanding of species-specific N dynamics in perennial orchards,
it is hypothesized that soil mineral N dynamics differ between apple and sweet cherry due
to species-specific plant–soil interactions. To capture these dynamics, this study compares
soil mineral N stocks (NO3−and NH4+) in the root zone of apple and sweet cherry orchards
under field conditions and evaluates whether differences can be attributed to environmental
factors, soil properties, management practices, tree species or other underlying drivers.
2. Materials and Methods
This study aimed to investigate species-specific differences in soil mineral nitrogen
(Nmin) stocks in apple (Malus domestica Borkh.) and sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.) or-
chards. Both study sites are located in regions with comparable soil and climate condi-
tions, ensuring the transferability of results between experiments. The work consisted of
two sequential steps: analysis of nitrate (NO3−) and ammonium (NH4+) stocks in soils
from a long-term orchard experiment (2020–2021), and a comparative study in a commercial
orchard (2022) to confirm species-specific patterns.
2.1. Study Sites
2.1.1. Long-Term Orchard Experiment (LTOE), Klein-Altendorf, Germany (50◦37′26′′ N
6◦59′33′′ E)
The long-term orchard experiment (LTOE) was established in 1968 on a 0.5 ha plot
at the Klein-Altendorf experimental station of the University of Bonn (Germany) [26].
Originally designed exclusively for apple orchards, the trial was established to assess
the long-term effects of different fertilization programs on soil fertility, fruit quality, and
marketable yield. In 2010, it was expanded to include sweet cherry orchards using the

The site is located at 160 m above sea level on a slight incline (0.5–1.0◦) and has
a Western Atlantic climate, with an annual mean temperature of 9.5 ◦C and ~600 mm
precipitation [27]. The soil is a fertile Luvisol on loess with a loamy silt topsoil and a more
clay-rich subsoil. The site scores >90 on the German soil evaluation scale (0–100) and has a
pH of 6.7 in limed plots and 5.7–5.9 in non-limed plots [28]. In the topsoil (0–20 cm), the
mean total organic carbon (TOC) content was 1.25% in apple plots and 1.11% in cherry plots,
while the mean total nitrogen (TN) content amounted to 0.15% and 0.13%, respectively. In
compost-amended plots, TOC contents were 3.03% in apple plots and 3.12% in cherry plots,
and TN contents were 0.34% and 0.32%, respectively.
Currently, the LTOE comprises two orchard sections: one planted with apple trees
(rootstock ‘Malling 9’, cv. ‘Jonagored’, since 2011) and one with sweet cherries (rootstock
‘GiSelA®5’, cvs. ‘Carmen’, ‘SPC 232’, ‘Henriette’, since 2010). Early modifications included
the use of different cultivars and a brief inclusion of pear trees, but the trial design and
management have remained consistent throughout the study period.
The fertilizer treatments are arranged horizontally across the orchard rows and com-
prise eight treatments. For this study, four treatments were selected:
•
Control: no fertilizer application;
•
Lime: 1 t ha−1 yr−1 CaO, applied at ~10-year intervals for pH stabilization;
•
Lime + compost: lime plus ~10 t ha−1 yr−1 dry mass of municipal green compost
(RETERRA®, Erftstadt, Germany);
•
Lime + NPK: lime plus 2–3 applications of 20 kg ha−1 yr−1 N, 30 kg ha−1 yr−1 P2O5,
and 45 kg ha−1 yr−1 K2O.
Compost was applied in spring as a 5 cm surface layer. Mineral fertilizers included
urea (N), superphosphate (P2O5), and potash (K2O). Cultivation practices followed inte-
grated pest management principles, including targeted herbicide applications for weed
control. Until 2009, 3 t ha−1 yr−1 of farmyard manure was applied, after which it was
replaced by compost in order to align with circular economy principles.

2.3. Analyses
Analyses of NO3−and NH4+ were carried out in an accredited laboratory (LKS
GmbH—Landwirtschaftliche Kommunikations-und Servicegesellschaft mbH, Niederwiesa,
Germany). The analysis involved extracting 40 g of frozen soil samples with 160 mL
of 0.0125 M CaCl2, using a four-channel SCALAR flow analyzer (Scalar Analytical B.V.,
Breda, The Netherlands). This was performed in accordance with German standard proce-
dures [31].

2.4. Data Analyses
Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 and R version
4.1.3. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted separately for each N form (NO3−,
NH4+), sampling date, and tree fruit species. To meet the assumption of normality required
for univariate ANOVA, data were logarithmically transformed prior to analysis. Instead of
a classical post hoc test, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the treatment
means. Treatments with non-overlapping CIs were considered significantly different and
assigned different letters. This CI-based approach was chosen because it provides additional
information on the precision of the mean estimates and the magnitude of differences. For
the fertilization factor (control, lime, lime + compost, lime + NPK), Tukey’s HSD post hoc
test was additionally performed. The assignment of letters was identical between the CI
method and Tukey’s HSD, confirming the robustness of the results. The significance level
was set at α = 0.05. Error intervals were not displayed in the graphical representations to
enhance visual clarity, although all statistical analyses were performed on the full dataset.
The figures were created using MS PowerPoint 365 (Version 16.70) and CorelDRAW®
Graphics Suite 2018.

4. Discussion
To interpret the observed temporal fluctuations in NH4+ stocks, it is essential to
consider how management practices, weather-related factors, and soil physicochemical
conditions influence Nmin stocks and their composition, while also considering potential
plant-mediated contributions. Higher NO3−and NH4+ stocks in the compost-treated plots,
together with increased TOC and TN, indicate enhanced mineralization of compost-derived
organic matter. The long-term application of compost likely contributed to the accumula-
tion of soil organic carbon and nitrogen pools, which in turn serve as a sustained source
for mineral N release. Against this background, differences in mineral N (NO3−, NH4+)
may at least partly be attributed to compost-driven mineralization processes, while con-
comitant changes in TOC and TN reflect the cumulative effects of repeated organic matter

inputs [9,33,34]. The lower NO3−and NH4+ stocks observed in 2021 compared with 2020
in Klein-Altendorf may likewise be explained by reduced organic matter mineralization
under the cooler and wetter conditions prevailing in 2021 [35,36].
The fruit species-specific shifts in NO3−and NH4+ stocks could not be fully explained
by established management practices such as fertilization or weather-related factors. In
apple orchards, Nmin was dominated by NO3−(approximately 83%), while in cherry
orchards, it was distributed more evenly between NO3−(approximately 42%) and NH4+
(approximately 58%) stocks. The lack of statistical significance may partly reflect the
lower analytical precision of NH4+ measurements compared with NO3−. While these
observations point to limitations of management-based and weather-related explanations,
visual inspection nevertheless suggests clear species-specific patterns in Nmin composition.
Figure 5 presents a bivariate scatterplot of NO3−and NH4+ stocks for apple and cherry
soils across both study sites. Soils from apple orchards were characterized by pronounced
variation in NO3−stocks accompanied by consistently low NH4+ levels, whereas soils
under cherry trees exhibited strong fluctuations in NH4+ stocks with comparatively lower
average NO3−values. This indicates that variability in Nmin composition is primarily
driven by NO3−in apple orchards and by NH4+ in cherry orchards, potentially reflecting
fruit species-associated differences in nitrification or ammonium retention processes.

indications of oxygen limitation or severe physical constraints such as soil compaction
were observed based on the measured soil characteristics [37–39]. These conditions pro-
vide limited evidence that physicochemical constraints alone account for the observed
NH4+ enrichment.

Given that management practices, weather-related factors and the measured soil
physicochemical conditions did not fully explain the observed patterns, plant-mediated
processes represent one possible explanation that warrants further investigation [40]. The
apple rootstock ‘M9’ and the cherry rootstock ‘GiSelA®5’ differ markedly in root architec-
ture, root system density, and rhizosphere development, and may consequently influence
microbial N transformations in distinct ways. The thicker and more extensive cherry root
system could potentially influence rhizosphere conditions and associated microbial N
transformations. However, the present study did not assess root traits, microbial commu-
nity composition, or nitrification activity directly, and therefore no mechanistic conclusion
can be drawn. Moreover, nitrification-inhibiting effects have been documented in other
species [41], including rice (Oryza sativa) [42], signal grass (Brachiaria humidicola) [43], Nor-
way spruce (Picea abies (L.) H. Karst.), and Nordmann fir (Abies nordmanniana (Steven)
Spach) [44], raising the possibility, though not yet supported by direct evidence, that
similar biochemical mechanisms contribute to the elevated NH4+ proportions observed
in cherry orchards. While such observations from other plant species demonstrate that
plant-mediated effects on nitrification are possible, the present data do not provide direct
evidence that comparable mechanisms operate in sweet cherry orchards. Consequently, the
proposed involvement of altered nitrification or NH4+ retention should be regarded as a
hypothesis rather than a demonstrated process.
The increase in NH4+ proportions under cherry from February (BBCH 00–01) to March
(BBCH 01) in Beesenstedt coincided with the transition from dormancy to early spring
root activity. This observation suggests a possible link between tree phenology and soil N
dynamics. However, because root activity and microbial N transformation processes were
not directly assessed, the mechanisms underlying this pattern remain uncertain. Sweet
cherry’s higher N demand per unit of harvested fruit compared to apple [22] may be
relevant in this context. However, whether differences in nutrient demand, root activity,
rhizosphere processes, or microbial transformations contributed to the observed NH4+
enrichment remains unresolved. The present study focused on the early phenological
phase of orchard development and therefore provides a comparative assessment of soil
mineral N composition at a defined stage rather than a characterization of seasonal N
dynamics. Future studies incorporating repeated sampling throughout the growing season
would help determine whether the observed species-specific differences persist across later
phenological stages and varying environmental conditions.
Taken together, these observations indicate that while seasonal conditions and fer-
tilization determine absolute Nmin levels, the relative composition of NO3−and NH4+
appears to be influenced by tree species. The consistently higher NH4+ proportions ob-
served under cherry trees suggest a species-associated pattern in soil N dynamics that has
not been documented previously in orchard soils. These results highlight the potential
importance of plant–soil interactions in shaping N cycling in perennial fruit systems be-
yond environmental and management-related controls. The consistency of species-specific
NH4+ enrichment across two independent orchard systems points towards a consistent
field pattern rather than site-specific artefacts. To distinguish between observed patterns
and their underlying causes, future studies should directly investigate the mechanisms
governing species-specific N dynamics in orchard soils. Potential approaches include
quantification of nitrifier and ammonifier communities using functional gene markers (e.g.,
amoA), characterization of root exudate composition, measurements of gross nitrification
rates using isotopic techniques, and assessment of rhizosphere-specific microbial activity.
Such analyses would help determine whether the elevated NH4+ proportions observed
under cherry trees result from reduced nitrification, enhanced NH4+ retention, altered plant
uptake patterns, or other rhizosphere-mediated processes.

5. Conclusions
This study indicates that soil mineral nitrogen composition differs between apple and
cherry orchards, with apple soils dominated by nitrate and cherry soils exhibiting a higher
proportion of ammonium. These differences were not consistently explained by year, site,
fertilization type, or the measured soil parameters. The higher proportion of ammonium in
cherry orchards suggests a species-associated pattern in soil N dynamics, while interactions
with environmental conditions, soil properties, and management practices cannot be
fully excluded. The consistency of this pattern across two independent orchard systems
indicates that fruit species may influence soil mineral nitrogen composition under field
conditions. However, the mechanisms underlying these differences remain unresolved.
Further research is required to identify the underlying biological and biogeochemical
mechanisms and to assess the broader applicability of these patterns across sites, soil types,
rootstocks, and phenological stages.
