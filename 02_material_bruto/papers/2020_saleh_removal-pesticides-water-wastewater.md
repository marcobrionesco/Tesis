a b s t r a c t
The levels of pesticides in water have increased due to their excessive use in the
modern agricultural domain. Choosing a suitable water treatment method for pesticide
removal depends on the type of pesticide and the efficacy of the treatment process.
This review critically discusses and provides a detailed description of a variety of
chemical, physical, and biological approaches for pesticide removal from water, such
as advanced oxidation processes, adsorption, activated sludge, membrane bioreactors,
and membrane technologies. Both single-treatment and hybrid methods are thoroughly
described and critically discussed. The use of hybrid removal techniques offers potential
opportunities to develop innovative pesticide removal techniques. A thorough analysis
of influent composition and recognition of the best removal technique is very important
to design a water treatment plant that targets the maximum possible removal rate for
different types of pesticides. Furthermore, the decentralization of water treatment was
also discussed in which it is an important approach that would lead to better effluent
water quality for lower prices. Many affordable techniques such as activated sludge and
adsorption by agricultural adsorbents showed high efficacy in treating high levels of
different pesticides.

1. Introduction
Anthropogenic practices and industrial outputs are leading to an increased level of emerging contaminants into the
natural environment (Arihilam and Arihilam, 2019). The issue of emerging contaminants is a public health concern and
it is widely studied around the world, yet, no final solutions have been suggested. Among the emerging contaminants,
pharmaceuticals (hormones, antibiotics, and others), cosmetics, synthetic dyes, and pesticides are of the major concern in
many areas around the world (Gomes et al., 2018). The worldwide production of these micro-contaminants has reached
around 500 million tons/year (Thomaidis et al., 2012). Furthermore, many pesticides are stable over time and, therefore,
can be transported in water and air to contaminate areas far from their point source (Lofrano et al., 2020). The sporadic
use of pesticides has affected ecosystems, which threaten birds, wildlife, domestic animals, fish, and livestock (Coppock
and Dziwenka, 2020).
Pesticides, for instance, have been proven in many studies as toxic substances to humans and the environment (Rasheed
et al., 2019). For example, chronic exposure to herbicides like atrazine (1-chloro-3-ethylamino-5-isopropylamino-2,4,6-
triazine) which acts as a selective herbicide that inhibits photosynthesis in susceptible plants, causes cardiovascular
problems, retinal degenerations, some muscles degeneration and cancer in human. Among herbicides, oxyfluorfen inhibits
protoporphyrinogen oxidase, leading to irreversible cell membrane damage. Human exposure to oxyfluorfen can cause
problems in the liver and the blood count (anemia) (EPA, 2002). Besides, among the environmentally concerning
pesticides, azoxystrobin, a broad-spectrum fungicide that inhibits fungal spore germination, is known to be highly toxic
to freshwater fish and estuarine (Sutherland and Ralph, 2019).
The increase in the world population from 1.5 to 6.9 billion between 1900 and 2000 has increased the demand for
food, which puts pressure on agricultural sectors. Modern agriculture utilizes pesticides to increase the productivity to an
economically profitable level (Kılıç et al., 2020). Rivers are also becoming highly polluted by industrial, agricultural, mining,
and urban wastewaters, leading to increased levels of pesticides from agricultural and landscaping origins (Mushtaq
et al., 2020). Additionally, washing tools and machines used in preparing pesticides and spraying them, as well as the
pesticide traces in gray water upon washing contaminated fruits and vegetables lead to the enrichment of wastewater by
pesticides (Manasa and Mehta, 2020). In addition to domestic wastewater, factories involved in chemicals and pesticide
production produce their own wastewater known as pesticides-production wastewater. Pesticides-production wastewater
requires excessive treatment before being mixed with the domestic wastewater. Therefore, pesticide treatment from water
sources is a crucial research domain due to their high concentrations and recalcitrance in wastewater (Stuart et al., 2012;
Köck-Schulmeyer et al., 2013; Goodwin et al., 2017; Vela et al., 2019).
Among the various challenges that face pesticide treatment from water is the influent composition, the variability of
the pesticides’ physical structures, and the pH of pesticides-contaminated water, which ranges from very acidic (0.5) to
very alkaline (14). Moreover, the chemical oxygen demand (COD) of pesticides-production wastewater ranges according
to the literature between 150 and 33750 mg/L while the biological oxygen demand (BOD) ranges between 30 and 11590
mg/L. The amounts of pesticides in various sources of water vary between 0.1 and 107 mg/L (Goodwin et al., 2017;
Rodriguez-Narvaez et al., 2017).
There are various treatment techniques for the removal of pesticides from water, which are thoroughly discussed
in this review. However, the current treatment techniques involve a combination of physical, chemical, and biological
methods have been utilized to instigate and elucidate the problem of the removal of pesticides from water. Each treatment
technique has its own advantages and limitations not only in terms of capital and operational costs, but also in terms
of efficiency, operability, reliability, environmental impact, pre-treatment requirements, and the production of sludge
and toxic byproducts. For instance, advanced oxidation process (AOPs) produces less sludge, rapid reaction rates, less
retention time, but it has a high capital, operational, and maintenance cost (Marican and Durán-Lara, 2018). Fenton’s
reaction can degrade soluble and insoluble dyes in the industrial effluents, does not form any bromated byproducts, has
a wide application range, and simple operation. It has some drawbacks that it requires low pH (2.5–4) which leads to
increased operating costs. However, it forms anions in high concentrations in the treated wastewater, as well as forming
a large amount of ferrous iron sludge (Goodwin et al., 2017). Another chemical treatment technique is photochemical
degradation which has no potential for the formation of bromated byproducts. However, it needs a separation step when
adding the catalyst as a slurry. Chlorination is a cheap and easy to implement chemical treatment technique, but the

2. Water treatment techniques for pesticides removal
Both the high emergence of water contaminants and the challenges that face their natural breakdown has led to the
development of many wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) techniques that are divided into chemical, physical, and
biological methods as indicated in Fig. 1.
2.1. Chemical treatment techniques
Chemical wastewater treatment consists of a variety of chemical reactions that help in hydrolyzing contaminants into
safer chemicals. The main chemical methods are coagulation and advanced oxidation process (AOPs), including ozonation
and Fenton treatment (Salimi et al., 2017). Chemical treatments are usually used in combination with photo-catalysis
and/or membrane techniques (Meghwal et al., 2019).
2.1.1. Iron-enhanced sand filters (IESFs)
Urban runoff or stormwater is a major source of contaminants. A study conducted by Fairbairn et al. (2018) on 36
stormwater samples from nine different sites from Minneapolis–Saint Paul Metropolitan in the USA has presented 123
types of contaminants including pesticides, pharmaceutical products, cosmetics, and others. Thirty-one of the identified
contaminants were detected in more than 50% of the samples. Upon filtration of the stormwater samples with IESFs,
which consist of conventional sand filters with around 5% iron filings, the removal rate of the contaminants was between
26% and 100%. However, when it comes to pesticides, some of the moderately hydrophobic types had a removal rate of
only 10 to 30%. Moreover, the study showed negative removal of some contaminants, including some pesticides. This
indicates the back-transformation of some derivatives into their toxic formula in the IESFs. Another study on stormwater

2.1.2. Chlorination
Chlorination is a chemical water treatment method used to put limits on bacterial growth in wastewater, which
helps in controlling odor and taste. Chlorination has the advantage of being cheap and easy to implement. However,
pre-chlorination can cause the oxidation of micro-contaminants into intermediate byproducts that could be more toxic
and less removable than the parent compounds (Ahmed et al., 2017). A study investigating chlorination as a pre-
treatment method of pesticides showed a negative impact. Li et al. (2016) have used chlorination prior to filtration using
powdered activated carbon with coagulation–sedimentation-filters (PAC-CSF). The target pesticides of the study were
four organophosphorus pesticides (OPPs), namely chlorpyrifos, diazinon, malathion, and tolclofos-methyl at an initial
concentration of 10 µM. The OPPs are insecticides that inhibit the acetylcholinesterase enzyme; causing prolonged muscle
contraction which kills insects. It is known that chronic exposure to OPPs can cause nervous system problems in both
humans and animals (Colović et al., 2013). The results showed that the pre-chlorination of OPPs oxidized them into more
toxic oxons that are hard to remove by PAC-CSF as shown in Fig. 2. Therefore, water contaminated with OPPs should not
be treated by chlorination in order to avoid removal complications (Li et al., 2016).
Kamel et al. (2009) have studied chlorination for the treatment of initial concentration of 100 ng/L of nine different
organophosphate pesticides from water including methyl parathion. The results showed that chlorination has led to the
formation of oxons and other oxidation products that are stable in water and require further toxicity tests. Another
study on chlorination has evaluated the removal of 44 pesticides, some showed positive removal and others did not.
Specifically, isoproturon, diuron, and parathion methyl were 100% removed, while only 20% of the atrazine were only
removed. Note that influent pesticides’ concentrations of isoproturon, diuron, and parathion methyl were 500 ng/L while
atrazine initial concentration was 551 ng/L (Ormad et al., 2008). The two contradictory conclusions that the two last-
mentioned studies came up with concerning parathion methyl could be due to the fact that the study did not check
for the oxidation byproducts, which are crucial whenever AOPs are used for treatment. As previously discussed, 100%
removal does not necessarily mean safe water. The latest studies showed that the treatment of water containing alachlor
by chlorination as a secondary treatment following oxidation helped in further oxidizing the pesticide (100 µg/L) by
the remaining persulfate from the primary treatment and the free chlorine. Alachlor is an herbicide that belongs to the
chloroacetanilide family and that inhibits weed growth by elongase inhibition. It is used to control grass and broadleaf
weeds in crops. Although the hybrid technique discussed in the study showed a reduction in the disinfection byproducts
(DBPs), yet further investigations were needed to reduce DBPs to an acceptable level (Wang et al., 2020).

2.1.3. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs)
Advanced oxidation processes consist (AOPs) of the usage of oxidizing agents to oxidize contaminants (Wang and
Zhuan, 2019). Among the strongest oxidizing radicals used is the hydroxyl radical (•OH). Sulfate radical (SO4•-) has
also been widely studied for the removal of organic contaminants from water (Tsydenova et al., 2015). AOPs are
environmentally friendly chemical techniques that can degrade organic contaminants into harmless products that neither
transfer contaminants from one phase to another and nor produce massive amounts of sludge. Furthermore, this technique
has various advantages, including rapid reaction rates leading to less retention time compared to other conventional
treatment techniques, and it does not require a large area for processing the needed flow rate for the system. However,
various drawbacks can also be highlighted, including its high operating, and maintenance costs. It also has a complex
chemistry tailored to specific contaminants which requires skilled personnel to design the system (Marican and Durán-
Lara, 2018). Fig. 3 shows the various AOPs methods applied in water treatment systems. The AOPs are divided, in general,
into ozone-based treatment, ultraviolet (UV)-based treatment, electrochemical advanced oxidation processes (eAOP),
catalytic advanced oxidation processes (cAOP), and photo advanced oxidation processes (pAOP). The most commonly
used AOPs in pesticide treatment is discussed in the following sub-sections.
2.1.3.1. Free radicals. Vela et al. (2019) have studied the efficiency of sodium persulfate (Na2S2O8) as oxidizing species
to remove 17 different pesticides (pymetrozine, flonicamid, imidacloprid, acetamiprid, cymoxanil, thiacloprid, spinosad,
chlorantraniliprole, triadimenol, tebuconazole, fluopyram, difenoconazole, cyflufenamid, hexythiazox, spiromesifen, folpet
and acrinathrin) of initial concentration in water between 0.02 and 1.17 mg/L. Persulfate should be activated to form
sulfate radicals, knowing that sulfate radicals have a longer lifetime than •OH which allows them to stay in contact with
the organic contaminants for a longer period. The ideal activation of persulfate is by UV light (245 nm) (Fig. 4). However,
due to feasibility and cost reduction reasons, sunlight was used instead. As shown in Fig. 4, persulfate photolysis was
used to produce (SO4•-) free radicals that have the ability to oxidize pesticides into less harmful substances, which will
ultimately lead to the mineralization of the parent pesticide into CO2 and water. The results showed that the initial
dissolved organic carbon (DOC) was reduced by 87%. Four of the seventeen tested pesticides showed a level above their
limit of detection in water. Therefore, it is believed that oxidation by sulfate radicals would be a promising method that
deserves further investigation.

pesticide atrazine from water polluted with 4 mg/L atrazine. This pesticide is, however, used to prevent broadleaf weeds
in crops including corn and sugar cane (Popova et al., 2019). Furthermore, Wang et al. (2019) have enhanced the function
of the oxidizing agent; magnesium dioxide (MnO2) by bisulfite (HSO−

2.1.3.2. Photochemical degradation. Ultraviolet (UV) light is electromagnetic radiation that is usually used for photodegra-
dation of contaminants in wastewater. UV light can be categorized into four categories based on its wavelength, namely
UV-A (315–400 nm), UV-B (280–315 nm), UV-C (180–280 nm), and vacuum UV light (10–180 nm). The lower the
wavelength; the stronger its effect. Various UV lamps have been used in wastewater treatment plants. However, UV light
is usually not enough on its own for contaminant degradation so it is used in combination with other techniques among
which photochemical degradation has been widely studied (Goodwin et al., 2017).
Triazophos is an organic thiophosphate or an organothiophosphate insecticide that protects cotton and rice crops from
aphids by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase. The residues of this pesticide are known to be toxic to the environment. The
photodegradation of triazophos (10 mg/L) in the presence of titanium dioxide (TiO2) as a catalyst has been studied and
showed promising results. The method is not costly as sunlight is available and titanium dioxide is not an expensive
chemical. The preliminary results showed an effective degradation rate, yet 17 degradation byproducts were encountered
which requires toxicity analysis. The method needs some improvement to achieve the total mineralization of triazophos
(Aungpradit et al., 2007). In a study conducted by Lee et al. (2017), Fe2O3(0.5)/TiO2 was used for the degradation of 2,4-
dichlorophenoxyacetic acid as illustrated in Fig. 5. Exposure of the photocatalyst to UV light leads to the excitement of
the photogenerated electron from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB) of the TiO2 and the photogenerated
holes are left in the VB. These holes oxidize 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid directly to 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) before
mineralizing it. The superoxide radicals are also formed due to the reduction of oxygen by the photogenerated holes.
Good charge transfer was promoted by the formation of heterojunction between Fe2O3 and TiO2 nanoparticles, which
also suppressed the electron–hole recombination.
As for carbofuran, one of the most toxic insecticides used in protecting potato, soybean, and corn crops by inhibiting
insects’ acetylcholinesterase, its removal using photocatalytic degradation in the presence of TiO2 or zinc oxide (ZnO)
as photocatalysts showed high removal rate. An experiment was conducted on influent water from 50 to 250 mg/L of
carbofuran. The results showed that removal in the presence of TiO2 was more effective than in the presence of ZnO.
Furthermore, monitoring the mineralization process by high-performance chromatography (HPLC) showed that during
the first hour, pesticides degraded into smaller fragments, which were later completely mineralized. The results proved
that photochemical degradation could be a very effective method in the mineralization of pesticides with similar chemical
structures (Mahalakshmi et al., 2007).

short lifetime, so it should be generated on-site, which increases the cost of the treatment. Among the studies conducted
on ozonation, Meijers et al. (1995) have tested the removal rate of 23 pesticides using this method. The results showed
efficient removal for six pesticides, namely dimethoate, chlortoluron, diuron, isoproturon, metoxuron, and vinclozolin.
However, when ozonation was accompanied by H2O2 oxidation, the results showed a good removal rate for all the tested

showed high removal rates when compared to other Fenton techniques with MCPA and mecoprop removal reaching 94%
(Ghanbarlou et al., 2020).

2.2. Biological treatment techniques
Various bio-purification systems (BPS) were designed to treat pesticides-containing wastewater using microorganisms
capable of digesting pesticides. Bio-mixtures are usually made of humic-rich substances to increase pesticide retention,
soil, and microorganisms. Bacteria present in the bio-mixture can be of endogenous or exogenous origins. Soil samples
collected from places rich with pesticides will have adapted microorganisms that would serve better as endogenous
biodegrading microorganisms. However, due to biomass limitations of some endogenous species, selected exogenous
species might be used (Karanasios et al., 2012). It is worth noting that it is not easy to digest pesticides using
microorganisms because of the toxicity of pesticides on various bacteria and fungi (Goodwin et al., 2017).
Biological treatment is divided into aerobic and anaerobic treatments. Among pesticides, the dichlorinated pesticides
are known to be digested by aerobic treatment. The process includes the oxidation and cleavage of the ether bond and the
hydroxylation of the chlorophenol to form chloro-catechol, and once the aromatic ring is open, the compound can be easily
digested by regular bacterial metabolism into water and carbon dioxide (Javaid et al., 2016). Di-chlorinated pesticides
can also be digested anaerobically by reductive dehalogenation. Once the amount of chlorine atoms is low enough, the
compound will be degraded into methane and carbon dioxide (Maltseva et al., 1996). Biological digestion of pesticides
is difficult, yet once established, the biodegradation system can be easily maintained. Pre-treatments are sometimes
necessary, for example, the breakdown of certain pesticides by photochemical degradation or enzymatic reactions might
facilitate their biological digestion (Huang et al., 2018). Zhao et al. (2017) have studied the enzymatic degradation of the
antibacterial agent norfloxacin (2 mg/L) from wastewater by chloroperoxidase (CPO); this reaction was triggered by the
addition of H2O2. The results showed that after 25 min reaction time, a degradation efficiency of 82.18% was achieved.
This degradation efficiency cannot be obtained with conventional treatment methods. Furthermore, this study determined
the eco-toxicity test by Chlorella pyrenoidosa and the test indicated that the norfloxacin-degraded products have lower
toxicity than the original norfloxacin molecule. In a recent study, aerobic biological treatment was adopted as a secondary
treatment after photocatalytic treatment of water contaminated with chlorpyrifos (insecticide), the results showed that
the biological treatment chosen had a high removal efficacy (Becerra et al., 2020).
2.2.1. Treatment of fungicides with a bio-mixture augmented with ligninolytic fungi
Ligninolytic fungi are fungi that secrete ligninolytic enzymes that depolymerize and mineralize lignin. Since some
pesticides are incorporated into lignin-based formulations, the addition of ligninolytic fungi to the bio-mixture that targets
pesticide degradation is a reasonable option. The matrix of such bio-mixture is usually made of humic-rich substances,
soil, microorganisms, and lignocellulosic substrate for the colonization of ligninolytic fungi (Castillo Mdel et al., 2008).
Murillo-Zamora et al. (2017) have studied the effect of BPS on seven fungicides (tebuconazole, carbendazim, metalaxyl,
triadimenol, Edifenphos, Epoxiconazole, and Fenbuconazole) after bioaugmentation with the ligninolytic fungi Trametes
versicolor. Fungicides belong to different groups of agrochemicals among which triazole is the largest in number and the
most persistent with a half-life in soil of 100 days. The results did not show any significant improvement of the fungicides
removal upon the addition of Trametes versicolor to the bio-mixture.

2.2.3. In-vitro treatment using microbial consortium
Hexachlorocyclohexane (HCH) is an insecticide made of a mixture of eight or more stereoisomers with an environmen-
tal half-life of 8 to 10 years. HCH belongs to the organochlorine pesticides. Although HCH is banned in many countries
due to its severe adverse effects, yet poorer regions still utilize them, causing an increase in the number of contaminated
sites. Furthermore, HCH is difficult to be eliminated from water. A laboratory-scale study conducted in India in 2007
has focused on developing a bacterial consortium capable of digesting HCH in liquid culture. The bacteria used in the
study were isolated upon enrichment of HCH contaminated sewage and soil. The most active consortium was made from
Pseudomonas, Burkholderia, Flavobacterium, and Vibrio strains (Murthy and Manonmani, 2007).
Atrazine is one of the problematic pesticides already discussed in this review. A study conducted on the biological
treatment of atrazine (0.01–10 mg/L) showed that submerged biological aerated filter (SBAF) containing a consortium
of adapting bacteria can remove atrazine from wastewater in a pilot-scale trial up to 97.9% (Baghapour et al., 2013).
Bacterial consortium with Diaphorobacter sp. strain LR2014-1 and Achromobacter sp. strain ANB-1 isolated from linuron
mineralization consortium was used to mineralize a combination of phenylurea herbicides (PHs). PHs are broad-spectrum
herbicides that inhibit photosynthesis, PHs are nowadays known as contaminants of coastal water, groundwater, and soil.
Toxicity analyses showed that some of the PHs are carcinogenic. The PHs included in this water purification method had
varied initial concentrations ranging from 4 to 56.4 µM, and they included: linuron, diuron, chlortoluron, fluometuron,
and siduron. The degradation percentages after 72 h were 99.9%, 94.3%, 96.4%, 53.5%, and 88.3%, respectively. Moreover, it
was noted that the consortium successfully degraded most of the herbicides byproducts (Zhang et al., 2018). Furthermore,
Sorensen et al. (2008) combined Arthrobacter globiformis strain D47 as diuron-degrading organism and Variovorax sp.
strain SRS16 as linuron-mineralizing organism to construct a linuron and diuron-mineralizing two-member consortium.
The results showed that a consortium consisting of both strains can remediate diuron and linuron in the contaminated
soil and water.
2.2.4. Pressurized activated sludge
Pressurized activated sludge is a highly efficient treatment technique that does not demand large land area, it is less
costly when compared to AOPs, and more environmentally friendly when compared to chlorination. However, it requires
a sludge disposal area as well as skilled labor for maintenance and operation (Luo et al., 2014). Among the limitations of
activated sludge is the difficulty of treating water with high COD in large volumes due to the limited amount of dissolved
oxygen under atmospheric pressure; the aeration process increases the technology cost. In the pressurized activated
sludge, the amount of soluble oxygen increases by enhancing total air pressure. The results showed more COD removal
with the increase of the operating pressure, aeration time, and sludge concentration to reach an optimum level of 85.0% to
92.5% COD removal (Jin et al., 2010). A study showed that the COD removal increased in such treatment with the increased
air pressure, aeration time, and sludge concentration. At 0.2 MPa pressure and 6 h aeration time, the COD removal of initial
concentration between 2500 and 5000 mg/L has reached to 92.5% (Pan et al., 2004).

2.2.5. Anaerobic–aerobic biological treatment
A laboratory-scale study that aimed to purify triadimenol from water has investigated the effectiveness of bioremedi-

2.2.6. Membrane bioreactor (MBR)
MBR is one of the latest technologies in wastewater treatment. It combines membrane filtration techniques with
biological treatment. Despite its high efficiency, it is worth noting that this technique requires high energy, in addition to
the cost of anti-fouling techniques needed to keep it functioning. An anoxic MBR showed within 15 h a removal rate of
65% for a broad spectrum herbicide known as ametryn (S-triazine) of initial concentration from 1 to 4 mg/L. Increasing the
retention time to 1.5, 2.5, and 7.5 days causes the removal rate to reach 83%, 92%, and 99%, respectively (Navaratna et al.,
2016). Another study conducted on organic contaminant removal by an anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) showed
that pesticides were difficult to be treated anaerobically with very low removal rates of atrazine (6.8%) and linuron (10.5%)
(Monsalvo et al., 2014).
In agriculture, pesticides are not the only chemicals used for pest control; antibiotics are also added to avoid bacterial
spoilage of crops. Therefore, pesticides-rich wastewater is also rich with antibiotics. Considering the mode of action
of antibiotics, this might limit the action of bio-remediating bacteria in a bio-mixture. Moreover, when choosing the
best bioremediation approach, the presence of antimicrobial agents with the rest of the pesticides should be taken
into consideration. Isolating adapted strains from soil already exposed to agricultural chemicals might guarantee the
presence of antimicrobial-resistant strains that are capable of digesting pesticides and probably antibiotics as well
(Castillo-Gonzalez et al., 2017).
2.3. Physical treatment techniques
Membrane filtration techniques are being widely used in wastewater treatment plants. Depending on the membrane
types and the target contaminants, filtration can be implemented at any stage during water treatment. The type of
filtration depends on the membrane cut-off size (Chiam and Sarbatly, 2011).
Nano-filtration membranes with pore sizes between 10−2 and 10−3 µm are ideal for the removal of bacteria, viruses,
and large organic molecules (Asad et al., 2020). Ahmad et al. (2008) have evaluated four different types of polyamides
nano-membranes for the filtration of dimethoate (an organophosphate insecticide) and atrazine. Both pesticides are
recognized as a public health concern and have their maximum limit in water set by the world health organization (WHO).
The lab-scale experiment assessed the filtration capacity of the tested membranes in which the amounts of contaminants
were tested by HPLC. The best membrane among the four tested membranes was named NF90 with a retention rate
of 85% for dimethoate and 95% of atrazine; the initial concentrations of the pesticides were tested at 2 and 20 mg/L,
respectively (Ahmad et al., 2008). Nano-filtration was proven in another study to separate the herbicide glyphosate from
saline wastewater (Song et al., 2013).

performance of the membrane. Results indicated that adding TEA to the membrane improved the rejection and water flux
as it was higher compared to the other membranes, where the rejection of diazinon and water permeability increased from
95.2% and 22 L/m2/h, respectively for the unmodified membrane to approximately 98.8% and 41.56 L/m2/h for the TEA
modified membrane. A significant improvement was indicated in poly (piperazine amide) TFC nanofiltration membranes
for the removal of pesticides. Similarly, Qin et al. (2020) used size-controlled spherical ZrO2 nanoparticles to prepare

performance was observed as the permeability of pure water was 3.9–4.2 L/m2/h. Regarding the removal of carbofuran,
it was found that the maximum removal rate was 89% with the ability to restore and reuse the membranes after alkali
wash and low-temperature calcination. In another study, Musbah et al. (2018) tested the use of 2 flat sheets negatively

2.4. Pesticide treatment using hybrid technologies
Most of the wastewater treatment studies focused on mixtures of one to four pesticides at a time, which is not the case
in real life. Furthermore, even when the removal rate reaches 99% using a certain single technique, the effluent might still
contain a total pesticides’ concentration that does not meet the standards of the Water Framework Directive (WFD) on
Environmental Quality Standards (EQS) set by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). As a result, it is important to
develop wastewater treatment plants that utilize combinations of physical, biological, and/or chemical treatment methods
to ensure that recalcitrant contaminants such as pesticides are lessened to the required limit before water discharge
(Goodwin et al., 2017).
Photochemical degradation might break down a contaminant into byproducts that are more biologically degradable.
Granular activated carbons could fixate contaminants in the mixture, which provides bacterial consortium more contact
with them and therefore more active degradation. Membrane bioreactors are widely used to mineralize some of the most
stressful contaminants (Goodwin et al., 2017).

tebuconazole, triadimefon, and pyrimethanil from municipal WWTP. The concentrations of the pesticides in the influent
water were 24, 16, 24, 16, and 20 ng/L, respectively. The treatment plant uses mechanical primary treatment, activated
sludge as secondary treatment, and sand filtration as tertiary treatment. The results found that, except for triadimefon
that had a removal rate of 93% after tertiary treatment, the remaining four pesticides showed removal rates below 50%.
The high-cost treatment processes were investigated and showed good removal rates for several tested pesticides,
especially diuron which is a carcinogenic potential herbicide that is highly persistent in an aqueous environment. The
treatment sequence used consisted of flocculation (coagulation), lamella clarification, filtration, and UV disinfection. Some
of the treated water was re-inject the water into the wells, while some were further treated by perchlorate, followed by
ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis, which have diuron levels that were within the acceptable range (Pitarch et al., 2010;
Teijon et al., 2010).

pyrimethanil. Pyrimethanil is a broad-spectrum fungicide that inhibits fungal enzymes, and it usually applies to seeds,
while alachlor is herbicide with a previously described mode of action. Traces of alachlor and pyrimethanil in the

2.4.2. Unsuccessful hybrid techniques
A study conducted by Köck-Schulmeyer et al. (2013) in Barcelona, Spain about the occurrence of 22 selected pesticides
and their removal rates in three different wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) showed poor removal rates. One of the
evaluated treatment plants adopted biological treatment only, while the second applied biological treatment followed
by removal of phosphorus and nitrogen, and the third one used biological treatment followed by tertiary treatments
(coagulation, flocculation, chlorination, and micro-filtration). The three Spanish WWTPs failed to remove the pesticides
encountered in the water. The trend of the removal of various pesticides was not clear, but it was worth noting that
triazines, atrazine, malathion, isoproturon, and bentazone showed the poorest removal rate with higher concentrations in
the effluent water. This study proves one more time that pesticides are particular contaminants of various characteristics
that cannot be removed by conventional treatment plants, and they require thorough research (Köck-Schulmeyer et al.,
2013).
Singer et al. (2010) have studied the removal rate of many pesticides, including diazinon, diuron, atrazine, desethyl-
atrazine, terbuthylazine, isoproturon and mecoprop in a WWTP that uses an activated sludge as secondary treatment
and sand filtration as tertiary treatment. The results showed a removal rate below 50% for all tested pesticides, except
mecoprop in which it showed a negative removal rate. Another unsuccessful fungicides (propiconazole and tebuconazole)
treatment methodology was discussed by Kahle et al. (2008) in which the treatment process of the contaminated
wastewater started with mechanical treatment followed by biological treatment using activated sludge and chemical
treatment using phosphate co-precipitation with iron salts. The results showed that the fungicides were not removed.
The negative removal of organic compounds in general and pesticides, in particular, can be explained by the fact
that some of these organic compounds get transformed in nature, or metabolized in the human digestive tract into
immeasurable products, that are sometimes converted back into their parents’ compounds when passing through the
treatment plant. This is supported by the levels of human daily intake of pesticides that are usually higher than the
recommended levels according to the monitoring system of the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) (USDA,
2020). This is also supported by studies on pesticide occurrence that show no significant difference between the levels
of pesticides on fresh produces before and after washing with water which proves human ingestion of these organic
contaminants (Jelic et al., 2011; Al-Shamary et al., 2016).
3. Decentralization of wastewater treatment systems for better pesticides removal
As it can be observed from this review, many full-scale wastewater treatment systems have failed in removing some
of the addressed pesticides, mainly because they are not designed to do so. Centralized treatment plants aim to improve
water quality by clearing major contaminants. However, pesticides, in particular, have varied chemical characteristics
that make them hard to be removed using conventional treatment techniques. Therefore, if a pesticide is treated
upon generation, the amounts of water that need to go through specialized treatment techniques would be much less
(Singh et al., 2014). For example, many of the pharmaceutics are concentrated in hospital wastewater, pesticides, and
fertilizers, although might end up in many water sources yet they occur in high concentration in agricultural wastewater,
while industrial wastewater is rich with chemicals that are hard to remove. Mixing hospitals, industry, and agricultural
wastewater with the domestic water will increase the volume of water that requires specific treatment methods. Having
small units specialized in treating every sector’s wastewater will not only save the money on water transportation to
a centralized treatment unit but also will ensure the usage of appropriate techniques depending on water composition
(Kovalova et al., 2012). Recent recommendations suggest having the effluent water directly employed on site. For example,
water contaminated with certain levels of pesticides should not be treated to zero levels, they can instead be directly used
in crop agriculture. This will save money twice by lowering the cost of water treatment and saving some of the money
spent on pesticides addition to the crops (O’Connor et al., 2008).

4. Comparative analysis
The usage of combined techniques is the best approach to design methods to remove and degrade environmental and
public health concerning contaminants such as pesticides. This review covered the treatment of many types of pesticides
from water. Seventy-six types of pesticides had their removal rate of water evaluated using different techniques; this
includes 25 herbicides, 27 insecticides, 20 fungicides, one bactericide, one algaecide, one molluscicide, and one nematicide.
It is worth noting that the studies involved in pesticide biological treatments focused on herbicides and insecticides, as
fungicides and bactericides might have a direct effect on the biomass of the bacterial consortium.
When different water treatment techniques were tested, the removal rates varied. It is very important to note that
removal rates are not enough to decide that the techniques are effective in water detoxification at full-scale trials.
Therefore, checking for toxic treatment byproducts, especially in the case of chemical treatments, is very important
before adopting a technique. In the case of atrazine, Fig. 12 shows the different removal percentages obtained with
the most effective techniques. Such a comparative removal analysis is of major importance during the selection of
treatment methods. The steps of the wastewater treatment plant establishment should include thorough influent water
analysis to determine contaminants, detailed literature review research to determine effective treatment techniques for
the encountered contaminants, and an overall feasibility study to determine the cost of each of the effective methods. For
instance, the best methods for atrazine removal were nano-ZnO, biological treatment using specific microbial consortium,
adsorption using wood charcoal and biochar in addition to ozonation/H2O2 and nano-filtration. To differentiate among
these six best methods in terms of removal rate, further research is required to test for the possible formation of DBPs.
It is also important to mention that the influent rate with atrazine was not the same in the six studies, the highest
amount removed was in the case of nano-filtration in which the initial rate was increased up to 20 mg/L. Among the
more affordable methods, the microbial consortium and the adsorption using biochar showed removal rates of 97.9 and
95.5%, respectively, with an atrazine initial level of 10 mg/L.
As for methyl parathion, studies show that removal by adsorption could be the most effective and most affordable
method. Chestnuts were the best adsorbent that removed efficiently with the highest initial rate (Fig. 13). The best
treatment method for 2,4-D was oxidation (peroxymonosulfate) followed by ozonation in the presence of peroxide, and
adsorption using wood charcoal (Fig. 13). Note that the highest influent rate treated was 20 mg/L in the case of oxidation
(peroxymonosulfate) method. Finally, various methods of diuron and isoproturon treatment were also illustrated and the
highest removal rate of these herbicides was seen using chemical treatments (ozonation and chlorination), note that the
initial level of the pesticide during those studies was not indicated, neither was the breakdown by-products. Different
adsorbents showed also full removal as shown in Fig. 13.
